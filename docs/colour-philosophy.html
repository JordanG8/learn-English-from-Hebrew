אנגלית מההתחלה · design system · rules 6–9

Loud where it counts, calm where it matters

The app was built to be impossible to misread. That worked — and it produced white cards, on a white page, under a white header. This is how colour gets added without spending any of it.

Rule 6, on this page: nothing below should stop making sense.
Colour is what makes a child want to come back. It is never what tells them what to do.

What was already true

Five rules govern every screen, and none of them are up for negotiation: one primary action per screen, 64px minimum touch targets, three redundant channels on every instruction — Hebrew text, an icon, and colour — Hebrew reading RTL with English isolated LTR, and nothing hidden behind a gesture.

A six-year-old cannot get that wrong. They also cannot get excited about it. The four rules below continue that list rather than replacing it, which is why they start at six.

The four rules

06

Colour is the fourth channel, never the first

Rule 3 already spends colour as one of three redundant channels. Everything added on top is a fourth thing — decoration that reinforces, never a channel that carries.

A child here may be six, may not read Hebrew fluently yet, and may be colour-blind — roughly one boy in twelve. A colour they cannot decode must never be the difference between finishing a lesson and being stuck.

The greyscale test

Put the screen behind filter: grayscale(1). If a child could still finish the step, the colour is decoration and the change is safe. If not, meaning has leaked into hue and belongs back in text or shape. The switch at the top of this page runs the same test on this document.

07

One loud thing per screen

Rule 1 makes the primary action the biggest thing on screen. Rule 7 makes it the most saturated thing too. The eye picks a winner by chroma before it picks one by area, so a screen with six colourful things has no primary action, whatever size the buttons are.

Everything that is not that one action lives in tints: soft surfaces carrying full-contrast ink.

The squint test

Squint at the screen. The most colourful shape must be the thing to tap.

08

Hue is identity, not state

Colour belongs to things that are something — this lesson, this letter, this word. A lesson keeps its hue for the life of the app, so moving from האות A to האות B visibly changes rooms, and the track reads as a journey instead of a spreadsheet.

Colour never belongs to things that happen: right, wrong, locked, done. Those keep the fixed semantic set — go, stop, star, warn — plus an icon plus a Hebrew sentence. No child is ever asked to learn what a colour means.

The separation rule

Every identity hue sits at least 25° from every semantic hue. The wheel below shows why that number exists.

09

Paint the room, not the furniture

Colour arrives as large, soft, low-contrast surfaces behind content: the page wash, a row tint, a card's top rim, a giant letter. Text, icons and borders stay full-contrast on near-white.

That is the whole trick for adding a lot of colour without touching legibility. A pale wash behind a paragraph costs nothing — body ink still reads at 15:1 on top of it. Tinting the paragraph itself would cost everything.

The floor

Every text-on-background pair clears 4.5:1. The six identity pairs were measured before they were written down; they land between 6.2:1 and 7.5:1.

The arc the state colours don’t use

The semantic colours already own part of the axis. An identity hue that lands near one of them stops being a name and starts being a verdict.

  • stop 25° — wrong answer
  • warn 65° — careful
  • star 85° — earned
  • go 150° — correct, continue
  • brand 265° — the app's own accent

Hatched bands are the 25° of daylight each state hue keeps on either side. Every identity hue falls outside all of them. Brand blue is marked but not fenced: it says this is the app, never you were right.

A hue is never used alone. It ships as --color-<name>-soft, a surface, and --color-<name>-ink, the only thing allowed on it — chosen together, measured together. Components take a token; they never invent a colour. If you catch yourself writing color: var(--tint) on text, you want --tint-ink. If the pair you need doesn't exist, add a pair, never a hex.

The keyboard stays furniture

The board is the one surface that gets no identity colour at all, and that is a rule rather than an oversight. It is a reference instrument a child is trying to memorise the shape of, so every coloured cap is a landmark competing with the one cap that matters — the highlighted key. Caps stay neutral, the highlight stays brand blue, and the finger-position colours remain the single exception, because they are a genuine teaching channel and they already carry a shape and a bilingual label alongside the colour.

Adding colour later

  1. Does it survive filter: grayscale(1)?
  2. Is the primary action still the most saturated thing on the screen?
  3. Is the hue describing what something is, never what happened — and at least 25° from every semantic hue?
  4. Is the colour behind the content rather than on the text?
  5. Is it a soft/ink pair from :root, measured at 4.5:1 or better?
  6. Is it on the keyboard? Then no.
